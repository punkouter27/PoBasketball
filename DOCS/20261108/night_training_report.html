1. Dribble: why it is still the blocker

To dribble, the athlete must slap the ball down with the palm on every bounce. Three different training set-ups were tried tonight. Each line below is one attempt; the horizontal axis is training experience (millions of simulated steps).

  • r2: original reward — Reward as of last night, started from the walking policy.
  • r3: + reach & touch reward — Adds a reward for bringing the palm to the ball and a bonus for each touch.
  • r4/r5: + ball-in-hand starts — Many training episodes start with the ball rising into the palm, so touches actually happen.
What the charts show. Every attempt rises in reward, but the touch and control lines fall. The policy learns to stand still and let the ball die, collecting the "stand nicely" points, because it never discovers the timing of a second touch after the ball bounces. Giving it the ball in hand (r4/r5) makes the first touch happen, but not the rhythm. More training time on the same set-up did not change this; it needs a different teaching method (see section 4).

2. Walking: hand-tuned reward vs AMP (learning style from motion capture)

4. Recommended next step

Teach the dribble rhythm from an example, not from reward alone. Three options, best first:
  1. Scripted hand-ball reference: a short procedurally generated dribble (hand follows a sine path synchronised with the ball) used as an imitation target for the right arm only; the legs keep the walking policy's balance.
  2. AMP with a dribble clip: the AMP trainer from section 2 already works; it needs a motion-capture dribble clip (not in the current data set).
  3. Phase signal in the task inputs: tell the policy when the next touch is due (appended input, zero-initialised, per the frozen-interface rule).
Scramble and shot stay parked until dribble passes.
